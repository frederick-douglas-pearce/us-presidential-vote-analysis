# References

Outside material the project's writing may cite: news coverage, status trackers for the
National Popular Vote Interstate Compact (NPVIC), and books and scholarship. It's collected
here so a later post on any platform (the blog, Medium, LinkedIn) starts from a checked list
rather than a fresh search.

Two rules govern every entry, the same ones that govern the series:

- **Check before you repeat.** An entry records what a source *reports*. Any claim a post
  takes from it is checked against the record or a primary source before it's published, and
  figures that move (the compact's electoral-vote total above all) are re-checked the day the
  post goes out.
- **Findings, not arguments.** Notes say what a source covers and how it relates to this
  project's record. They don't rate a source's politics or argue with its author.

Entry fields: citation and date; **Reports** (what the piece covers, who it quotes, what data
it cites); **Checked here** (anything this project verified against its own record, with the
date); **Useful for** (posts or topics it could support).

### Levy and Berman, "America Finally Has the Votes to End the Electoral College" (2026-11-05)

Pema Levy and Ari Berman, ["America Finally Has the Votes to End the Electoral
College"](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2026/11/midterms-electoral-college-popular-vote-compact/),

### Kenning, "Could Democrats change how Americans vote for president?" (2026-11-06)

Chris Kenning, ["Could Democrats change how Americans vote for
president?"](https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/politics/elections/2026/11/06/democrats-elect-president-popular-vote-electoral-college/92631637007/),
*USA TODAY*, updated Nov. 6, 2026.

- **Reports:** after the 2026 midterms, Democrats hold the legislature and governorship in
  Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin, which could carry the compact past
  270 and put it in force as early as 2028. Gives the compact's current standing (18 states and
  DC, 222 electoral votes, Virginia the most recent, in April 2026) and its open legal question,
  whether Congress must consent under the Compact Clause. Quotes Alexander Keyssar (Harvard),
  Derek Muller (Notre Dame), Edward Foley (Ohio State) and Harry Roth (Save Our States). Cites
  a 2024 Pew Research Center survey on popular-vote preference by party, and recalls earlier
  setbacks: Nevada's 2019 veto and a failed 2023 bill in Michigan.
- **Checked here (2026-11-06):** the compact's [state-status
  page](https://www.nationalpopularvote.com/state-status) confirms 222 electoral votes across 19
  jurisdictions, with Minnesota already among them. NCSL's results roundup (above) counts six
  new Democratic trifectas, adding Arizona to the article's five; results are unofficial until
  certified. Using the 2024 allotments served by this project's API (in force through 2028),
  the five non-members among them are Arizona 11, Michigan 15, Nevada 6, Pennsylvania 19 and
  Wisconsin 10. All five would bring the compact to 283. Pennsylvania and Michigan are each
  necessary: without Pennsylvania the total is 264, without Michigan 268. With both, any two of
  Arizona, Wisconsin and Nevada are enough, and any one alone is not. The article's four
  non-members without Arizona reach 272.
- **Useful for:** a dated update to *222 Votes Away*; a dashboard announcement; any post that
  touches the compact's path to 270.

## Surveys and polling

Newest first.

### DeSilver and Gramlich, "Most Americans favor big changes to the political system that likely would require amending the Constitution" (2026-07-28)

Drew DeSilver and John Gramlich, ["Most Americans favor big changes to the political system
that likely would require amending the
Constitution"](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/07/28/most-americans-favor-big-changes-to-the-political-system-that-likely-would-require-amending-the-constitution/),
Pew Research Center, July 28, 2026.

- **Reports:** a roundup of earlier Pew surveys on reforms that would likely need a
  constitutional amendment. It brings in no new Electoral College survey: the popular-vote
  figures (63% change, 35% keep; Democrats 80%, Republicans 46%) are the Aug.–Sept. 2024 survey
  in the Kiley entry below. What it adds is the full trend: 61/35 in November 2000, 51/44 in
  November 2016 (the low point), 55/43 in January 2021, 63/35 in 2022, 65/33 in 2023 and 63/35
  in 2024 (change/keep, by survey date). Pew notes that surveys before 2020 were by telephone and
  asked about "amending the Constitution" rather than "changing the system". The other
  proposals, each from a 2023 survey or earlier: congressional term limits (87%), maximum age
  limits for elected officials in Washington (79%) and for Supreme Court justices (74%), limits
  on campaign spending (72%), and the Equal Rights Amendment (78%, 2020).
- **Checked here (2026-11-06):** read from Pew's page; public opinion isn't in this project's
  record, so there's nothing to check against.
- **Useful for:** the most recent Pew citation for popular-vote preference, with the trend from
  2000 on one page. When citing the figure, give the 2024 survey date, not this article's.

### Kiley, "Majority of Americans continue to favor moving away from Electoral College" (2024-09-25)

Jocelyn Kiley, ["Majority of Americans continue to favor moving away from Electoral
College"](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2024/09/25/majority-of-americans-continue-to-favor-moving-away-from-electoral-college/),
Pew Research Center, Sept. 25, 2024. The Kenning article above cites it.

- **Reports:** a survey of 9,720 U.S. adults on Pew's American Trends Panel, fielded Aug. 26
  to Sept. 2, 2024. 63% prefer that the candidate with the most votes nationwide win; 35% would
  keep the Electoral College. About eight in ten Democrats and Democratic leaners prefer a
  popular vote. Republicans and Republican leaners split 53% for keeping the Electoral College
  and 46% for replacing it. Majorities in every age group favor a change (66% under 50, 59% at
  50 and over). The partisan gap has lasted more than two decades: two-thirds or more of
  Democrats have favored a popular vote since the 2000 election, and Republican support was 27%
  in November 2016 and 46% at this survey. It updates Pew posts from 2021, 2022 and 2023.
- **Checked here (2026-11-06):** the figures above are read from Pew's page. They're public
  opinion, which this project's record doesn't measure, so there's nothing to check them
  against here. The 2016 and 2024 elections the trend brackets are in the record.
- **Useful for:** setting context for a post on the compact or the hybrid; showing that a
  question the record answers is one the public already has views on. Cite the survey's date,
  since opinion figures move.

## Books and scholarship

- Alexander Keyssar, *Why Do We Still Have the Electoral College?* (Harvard University Press,
  2020). The history of reform attempts, including the 1969–70 amendment that passed the House
  and died in the Senate. Keyssar is quoted in the Kenning article above. Not yet read; the
  interview below is the readable account of its argument.
  - **Companion interview:** ["Alex Keyssar's *Why Do We Still Have the Electoral
    College?*"](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/behind-the-book/alex-keyssar-why-do-we-still-have-the-electoral-college),
    *Behind the Book*, Harvard Kennedy School, Sept. 8, 2020. A video interview hosted by
    Alessandra Seiter, with a transcript on the page.
  - **Reports:** Keyssar gives three reasons the system has outlasted every attempt to replace
    it. *Complexity:* changing one part moves the rest, as when the Twelfth Amendment (1804) fixed
    the tie problem but entrenched parties. *Partisanship:* dominant state parties adopted
    winner-take-all to maximize their weight, and hostility to the system sharpened after 1824,
    when the House chose John Quincy Adams. *Slavery and its legacy:* a national popular vote was
    proposed in the Senate in 1816 (Abner Lacock of Pennsylvania) and dropped over Southern
    objections that it would forfeit the electoral weight the Three-Fifths clause gave enslaved
    people, and after Reconstruction the disenfranchising South kept its full electoral weight.
    Support for a popular vote after World War II was broad and bipartisan, and in 1969 the House
    passed an amendment by a supermajority before a filibuster by Southern senators killed it in
    1970. He finds that small states only occasionally blocked reform, contrary to the usual
    explanation. The compact then stood at 15 states (it's 18 plus DC now).
  - **Checked here (2026-11-06):** in this project's record, Jackson led the 1824 electoral
    vote with 99 to Adams's 84, so Adams took office from second place, as the interview says.
    The rest is history the record doesn't cover.
  - **Useful for:** the history behind *222 Votes Away* and any post on 1824 or the 1969–70
    amendment; the small-states finding cuts against a common assumption, and the series'
    apportionment tables can show how much weight small states actually carry.
- Michael Geruso, Dean Spears and Ishaana Talesara, [*Inversions in US Presidential Elections:
  1836–2016*](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26247), NBER Working Paper 26247 (2019). An academic
  treatment of the comparison this project makes: how often the Electoral College and popular
  vote disagree.
- Max Farrand, ed., *The Records of the Federal Convention of 1787*, vol. 2. Primary source on
  the framers' design debates.
- Alexander Hamilton, *The Federalist* No. 68. Primary source; the contemporary defense of the
  design.
- Tara Ross, *Enlightened Democracy: The Case for the Electoral College*. The best-known
  book-length defense, for balance.
